Running Neural Networks on a 1981 Processor: The Retro AI Project That Shouldn't WorkshareCopied!

*A developer implements autoregressive language model inference on the 6502 chip, blurring the line between archaeology and engineering.*
▲ 111 pts  ·  10 comments

In the category of achievements that are equal parts audacious and slightly unhinged: an engineer has successfully run an autoregressive language model on the 6502 processor, the 8-bit chip that powered the Commodore 64 and Apple II. The project isn't practical in any conventional sense. It's an act of computational archaeology—a deliberate exploration of how small the substrate can be before inference becomes impossible.

What's remarkable isn't that it works—it does, albeit slowly—but what the working implementation reveals about the nature of neural networks themselves. The 6502 has roughly 64 kilobytes of memory. Modern language models operate in the megabytes-to-terabytes range. Yet the fundamental operations—matrix multiplication, nonlinear activation, token sampling—remain fundamentally identical. By stripping away acceleration hardware, custom libraries, and architectural optimizations, the project exposes the raw algorithmic skeleton of what an LLM actually is: a series of mathematical operations that care nothing about the substrate they run on.

The modest 111 points and sparse 10 comments suggest a niche audience, but the project captures something that matters: it's a proof that the boundary between retro computing and cutting-edge AI is conceptually thinner than it appears. In an era of increasingly specialized silicon and massive training budgets, there's quiet subversion in proving that the core of modern intelligence can fit on chips that predate the personal computer revolution.
